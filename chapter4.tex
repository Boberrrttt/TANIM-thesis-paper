	\section{Machine Learning Model for Crop Recommendation}
	
	This section presents the development, training, and evaluation of the LightGBM-based crop recommendation model integrated into the TANIM system. It covers the data preprocessing and augmentation procedures applied to the Department of Agriculture - Region 10 Regional Soils Laboratory dataset, the performance metrics achieved across ten crop categories, and the feature importance analysis that identifies the soil parameters most influential in driving accurate crop predictions. The results demonstrate the model's capability to deliver	reliable and agronomically sound recommendations based on real-world soil health data collected from farming communities in Bukidnon.
	
	\subsection{Data Analysis Results}
	
	Data preprocessing involved consolidating multiple data sources into a single structured training dataset suitable for machine learning. The primary source was the Consolidated Soil Info RSL 2023--2025, a collection of historical soil records from the Department of Agriculture Region 10 containing verified soil profiles linked to crops previously cultivated on specific farms across Bukidnon. Each record in this dataset provides measurements for key agronomic parameters, including soil pH, organic matter percentage, phosphorus concentration in parts per million, and potassium concentration in parts per million, along with the corresponding zoned target crop assigned to that farm plot. These historical records were supplemented with simulated IoT sensor samples to increase dataset coverage and ensure that the training data reflects a broader range of soil conditions that may be encountered during actual field deployment. Prior to merging the sources, all irrelevant metadata, administrative annotations, and non-agronomic text fields were removed so that each final entry in the unified table consists exclusively of the soil parameter values and a single target crop label. This cleaning step was essential for ensuring that the model learns only from agriculturally relevant inputs without interference from extraneous information.

		\centering
		\caption{Data Distribution Comparison Between Original and Augmented Datasets}
		\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/DataAugmentation.pdf}
		\label{fig:data_augmentation}
	\end{figure}
	
	Figure \ref{fig:data_augmentation} shows the class distribution of both the original and augmented datasets side by side, with red bars representing the original sample counts and green bars representing the augmented counts for each crop class. The original dataset reveals a severe class imbalance, with Corn dominating at 2,462 records and accounting for 64.3 percent of the total 3,830 soil samples. In contrast, minority crop classes such as sweet pepper, ampalaya, and tobacco each contributed fewer than 75 records, representing less than 2 percent of the total data individually. Other underrepresented classes, including eggplant, sorghum, squash, and tomato similarly held very low sample counts relative to corn. This disparity between the majority and minority classes confirms that the raw Bukidnon soil dataset alone was insufficient for training a fair and unbiased crop recommendation model, as a classifier trained on such skewed data would be predisposed to favor the majority class and produce unreliable predictions for underrepresented crops.
	
	To resolve this imbalance, K-Means SMOTE was applied to the original dataset. The augmentation successfully expanded the total dataset to 24,620 records, with all 10 crop classes holding exactly 2,462 samples each. A total of 20,790 synthetic soil samples were generated across the 9 minority classes, while Corn retained its original count as the reference threshold. As visible in the green bars of Figure \ref{fig:data_augmentation}, every crop class reaches the same sample count after augmentation, confirming that K-Means SMOTE effectively produced a balanced and representative training dataset. By ensuring equal class exposure during model training, the risk of majority class bias was eliminated, which directly contributed to the improved and consistent per-class accuracy results observed in subsequent model evaluations.
	
	\subsection{Crop Recommendation Validation}
	
	The validation of the rule-based recommendation engine of the TANIM system was conducted by comparing system outputs against established agricultural standards and expert guidelines provided by the Department of Agriculture and the Bureau of Soils and Water Management. To ensure the reliability and agricultural accuracy of the system, the validation process focused on verifying the translation of qualitative Soil Test Kit results into quantitative limits. This included crop-specific fertilizer rates, modes of application protocols, and soil pH preference modeling.
	
	The core fertilizer and crop suitability logic of the system is based entirely on protocols from the Bureau of Soils and Water Management. Validation was performed by ensuring that the programmed logic strictly follows these published standards, thereby eliminating arbitrary decision-making within the recommendation engine. The system recommendations were cross-checked against reference manuals to confirm that every combination of soil sensor data results in agriculturally sound outputs.
	
	A critical aspect of the validation involved converting qualitative color-based Soil Test Kit results into actionable quantitative limits. The classification logic of the system was validated against official quantitative value references established by the Bureau of Soils and Water Management, covering the three primary macronutrients and their respective testing methodologies.

	Table~\ref{tab:soil_nutrient_reference} presents the official quantitative thresholds used as the basis for validating the system's nutrient classification logic. Nitrogen is assessed through Organic Matter (OM) percentage, with values at or below 1.7\% classified as Low, values from 1.7\% to 3.0\% as Medium, and values exceeding 3.0\% as High. For Phosphorus, the system applies a dual-method approach depending on soil pH: the Bray 1 method is used for acidic to neutral soils with a pH below 7.3, while the Olsen method is applied for alkaline soils with a pH of 7.3 or above. Potassium is evaluated directly in parts per million across all soil types, with thresholds set at 117 ppm and 235 ppm to delineate the Low-to-Medium and Medium-to-High boundaries, respectively. These thresholds served as the reference standard for confirming that the system's interpretation of soil sensor data aligns with scientifically accepted agricultural practices and BSWM protocols.
	
	
	
	\subsection{Feature Importance Analysis}
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\caption{Feature Importance Analysis Measured by Information Gain}
		\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/FeatureImportance.pdf}
		\label{fig:FeatureImportance}
	\end{figure}
	
	Figure \ref{fig:FeatureImportance} shows the feature importance analysis of the LightGBM model measured by information gain, revealing which soil conditions most strongly influence crop viability predictions across the ten target crop classes. Potassium concentration ranked as the single most important feature, recording the highest information gain value of approximately 460,000, which reflects the high biological demand for potassium among the target crops, particularly vegetables such as tomatoes, eggplants, and sweet peppers that consume significantly larger amounts of this nutrient compared to staple grains. Soil pH ranked second, functioning as a natural gatekeeper that determines whether plant roots can effectively absorb the nutrients present in the soil, making it a critical conditioning factor that interacts with all other nutrient parameters.
	
	Beyond the raw soil measurements, the engineered interaction features contributed substantially to the model's predictive capacity. The K\_pH\_interaction feature, which captures the combined relationship between potassium levels and soil acidity, ranked third in importance with a gain value close to that of pH itself, confirming that the interplay between these two parameters carries more predictive information than either variable alone. Organic matter percentage ranked fourth, followed by the P\_OM\_ratio, P\_ppm, and P\_K\_ratio features, all of which contributed comparable levels of information gain. The OM\_pH\_interaction feature ranked last among the selected features but still contributed meaningfully to the overall model. Collectively, the four engineered ratio and interaction features accounted for over 42 percent of the total predictive power, confirming that modeling complex nutrient interactions rather than relying solely on isolated measurements is essential for capturing the agricultural relationships that drive accurate and reliable crop recommendations.

This configuration successfully meets the minimum input requirement of the solar charge controller, which regulates the incoming power and ensures proper battery charging. Energy is stored in four 18650 lithium-ion batteries configured to supply at least 12V, enabling stable operation of all components.
	
	A buck converter was implemented to reduce the output voltage from the charge controller to 10V, which serves as the primary input for the ESP32 expansion board. This ensured a consistent and reliable power supply throughout system operation.
	
	For data acquisition, the ZTS-3001-TR-ECMOISTPHTEMPNPK-N01 soil sensor was used to measure seven parameters: soil moisture, temperature, pH, electrical conductivity, and nutrient levels (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium). The sensor operated within its required voltage range and produced stable readings when supplied with at least 12V. Wireless data transmission was carried out using the SX1278 LoRa module (433 MHz), which operated at 3.3V and enabled long-range communication. All modules were properly interfaced through the ESP32's GPIO pins.
	
	An I2C LCD was integrated to display real-time sensor readings. The display operated at 5V and communicated with the ESP32 via the I2C protocol, providing immediate feedback during system testing.

	\subsection{Fertilizer Recommendation}
	
	The validation of the rule-based fertilizer recommendation engine of the TANIM system was conducted through a systematic verification process that assessed the correctness of the computational pipeline, from initial soil nutrient classification to the generation of actionable fertilizer prescriptions. All validation benchmarks were derived from the published standards of the Bureau of Soils and Water Management (BSWM) and the Department of Agriculture to ensure that every system output is sound in terms of agricultural practice.
	
	The first phase of validation involved the NPK thresholding and grading module, which classifies raw soil sensor data into qualitative nutrient levels. The system categorizes Nitrogen using Organic Matter (OM) percentage, where values at or below 1.7\% are graded as Low, values from 1.7\% to 3.0\% as Medium, and values exceeding 3.0\% as High. A critical design decision validated during this phase was the conditional Phosphorus testing method: for soils with a pH below 7.3, the system applies the Bray 1 standard, whereas for alkaline soils with a pH of 7.3 or above, it switches to the Olsen method. Potassium is evaluated directly in parts per million, with thresholds set at 117 ppm and 235 ppm for the Low to Medium and Medium to High boundaries, respectively. The system outputs were verified against the BSWM reference tables to confirm that every soil input combination produced the correct nutrient grade assignment.

	\subsection{Usability Evaluation}
	
	This subsection presents a rigorous usability evaluation of the developed system, employing a structured, user-centered assessment framework to measure its effectiveness, efficiency, and user satisfaction across identified user groups. The evaluation was conducted using purposefully selected participants, consisting of a total of ten (10) respondents, specifically seven (7) farmers as primary end-users and three (3) Department of Agriculture (DA) officials as administrative users. This 7 to 3 distribution ensures adequate representation of both operational and managerial perspectives in system interaction.
	
	The farmer participants possess varying levels of agricultural experience ranging from a few years to over two decades of active farm management. Rather than relying solely on personal intuition, they generally follow established guidelines to direct their farming methods, applying a combination of personal estimates and formal agricultural recommendations for fertilizer application. Over the years, the group has collectively cultivated a variety of crops, including corn, cassava, sweet potatoes, and bananas, and each participant currently focuses their time and resources on managing a single farm. This profile reflects a typical smallholder farmer demographic in the Bukidnon region, providing a representative basis for evaluating the usability of the TANIM mobile application in its intended operational context.
	
	The Department of Agriculture respondents bring a different but equally critical perspective. The participating Department of Agriculture officials has been serving the institution since 2014, reflecting over a decade of professional experience in the agricultural sector. This extensive tenure indicates strong familiarity with the department's programs, policies, and field operations, particularly in providing technical assistance to farmers and implementing regional agricultural initiatives. In terms of professional role, the respondent holds the position of Senior Agriculturist, indicating a higher level of responsibility within the organization. Their primary area of focus encompasses crop production, soil management, and technical advisory services to farmers. This background makes the Department of Agriculture respondents well-qualified to assess the TANIM web application's administrative interface, particularly its soil data visualization, fertilizer management, and farm monitoring functionalities.
	
	The usability of the TANIM system was evaluated using the System Usability Scale (SUS), a standardized ten-item questionnaire widely recognized for its reliability in assessing perceived usability across diverse user groups and system types. Each respondent completed the SUS survey following a structured interaction session with the system, and individual scores were computed using the standard SUS scoring procedure.
