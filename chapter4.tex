\section{Hardware System}
This section presents the results of the sensor hardware testing and calibration conducted to evaluate the accuracy, reliability, and consistency of the developed IoT-based soil sensing unit. As described in Chapter 3, the system measures key soil parameters including pH, nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), soil moisture, temperature, and salinity. The accuracy of these parameters is critical since they directly influence the crop recommendation and fertilizer advisory outputs of the system.

The testing phase aimed to (1) verify the correctness of sensor readings, (2) calibrate the sensors against reference values, and (3) evaluate the performance of the sensing unit under controlled conditions. The results obtained from this phase serve as the foundation for validating the reliability of the entire system.

\subsection{Hardware and Specifications}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\caption{Soil Health Monitoring Device}
	\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/FinalHardwareSystem.pdf}
	\label{fig:hardware_system}
\end{figure}	

Figure \ref{fig:hardware_system} presents the developed system that utilizes three 5V, 1A solar panels connected in series, generating approximately 15V. This configuration successfully meets the minimum input requirement of the solar charge controller, which regulates the incoming power and ensures proper battery charging. Energy is stored in four 18650 lithium-ion batteries configured to supply at least 12V, enabling stable operation of all components.

A buck converter was implemented to reduce the output voltage from the charge controller to 10V, which serves as the primary input for the ESP32 expansion board. This ensured a consistent and reliable power supply throughout system operation.

For data acquisition, the ZTS-3001-TR-ECMOISTPHTEMPNPK-N01 soil sensor was used to measure seven parameters: soil moisture, temperature, pH, electrical conductivity, and nutrient levels (nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium). The sensor operated within its required voltage range and produced stable readings when supplied with at least 12V. Communication between the sensor and the microcontroller was achieved using a TTL-to-RS485 module, which successfully converted RS485 signals into 3.3V TTL signals compatible with the ESP32.

Location tracking was implemented using the GY-NEO6MV2 NEO-6M GPS module, which provided accurate latitude and longitude data during testing.

The ESP32 microcontroller, paired with an expansion board, served as the central processing unit. The expansion board accepted a 7V–12V input, while internally regulating power for the ESP32. Wireless data transmission was carried out using the SX1278 LoRa module (433 MHz), which operated at 3.3V and enabled long-range communication. All modules were properly interfaced through the ESP32’s GPIO pins.

An I2C LCD was integrated to display real-time sensor readings. The display operated at 5V and communicated with the ESP32 via the I2C protocol, providing immediate feedback during system testing.

\subsection{Accuracy and Reliability Evaluation}
To assess the accuracy and reliability of the calibrated soil health sensor under actual field conditions, validation testing was conducted using soil samples collected from farms in Libona and Lingion, Bukidnon. These locations were selected based on farm data provided by the Department of Agriculture - Region 10, ensuring representative soil conditions across the region. The validation process involved cross-checking calibrated sensor readings against two reference standards: the Department of Agriculture's Soil Test Kit (STK) results and laboratory analysis results obtained from the Department of Agriculture's Regional Soils Laboratory using the same farm data samples they provided form multiple farms.

Three soil samples from Libona Bukidnon were analyzed using the Soil Tests Kits (STK) methodology (see Appendix \ref{app:stk_results}), while three additional samples from Lingion Bukidnon were processed through the Department of Agriculture laboratory as part of their ongoing soil monitoring program. The laboratory reference values used in this comparison were extracted directly from the Department of Agriculture Region 10's soil database, ensuring consistency with established regional soil health records. This dual-reference approach provided both qualitative (color-based) and quantitative validation of the sensor performance across different soil types and nutrient conditions found in the target agricultural areas.

The evaluation focuses on four key soil parameters: Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K), and pH, and classifies the level of agreement into Exact, Partial, and Divergent categories to determine the reliability of the sensor outputs in both field-level and laboratory contexts.

For the STK vs Sensor comparison, the results indicate generally strong alignment across most parameters. Nitrogen shows a mixed performance, with both Exact and Partial agreements across samples, suggesting minor variability in sensor estimation but still within acceptable categorical proximity. Phosphorus demonstrates consistently high agreement, achieving Exact matching in two out of three samples, indicating that the sensor closely replicates STK-based interpretations for this nutrient. Potassium exhibits more variability, with one instance of Partial agreement and two Exact or near-Exact classifications, reflecting moderate sensitivity to environmental fluctuations in potassium measurement. Meanwhile, pH consistently achieves Exact agreement across all samples, confirming high stability and strong calibration accuracy of the sensor for acidity measurement when compared to STK results.

In contrast, the Lab vs Sensor comparison reveals a more challenging alignment due to the higher precision and quantitative nature of laboratory measurements. Nitrogen shows a combination of Partial and Exact agreement, indicating that while the sensor can approximate laboratory trends, minor deviations in absolute concentration values still occur. Phosphorus consistently records Divergent outcomes across all samples, suggesting significant discrepancies between sensor readings and laboratory measurements, likely due to differences in detection sensitivity or calibration scale. When compared to DA laboratory results from Lingion samples, the sensor showed consistent accuracy for pH measurements (100\% exact agreement) but revealed systematic differences in nutrient readings. The phosphorus measurements showed the greatest divergence, with the sensor consistently reading higher values than laboratory results. This pattern suggests potential calibration drift or methodological differences between sensor chemistry and laboratory analytical procedures. Potassium also shows predominantly Partial agreement, indicating that while general trends are captured, numerical precision remains limited compared to laboratory standards. Despite this, pH continues to maintain Exact agreement across all samples, reinforcing its reliability as the most stable and accurately measured parameter within the system.

Table \ref{tab:validation_summary} demonstrates that the proposed sensing system performs strongly in categorical classification, particularly for pH and partially for Nitrogen, while showing moderate limitations in accurately matching laboratory-grade precision for Phosphorus and Potassium. However, the consistent occurrence of Exact and Partial agreements suggests that the system is still effective for decision-support purposes in agricultural applications, where categorical interpretation is often more critical than exact numerical equivalence.

\section{Machine Learning Model}
This section describes the data processing pipeline and predictive model used for crop recommendation. The model takes processed soil measurements as input and outputs a recommended crop and fertilizer guidance.

\subsection{Data Integration and Cleaning}
Multiple data sources were consolidated to form the training dataset. Historical records (the Consolidated Soil Info RSL 2023–2025) provided soil profiles (pH, organic matter \%, phosphorus ppm, potassium ppm) linked to crops previously grown on those farms. These records were combined with simulated IoT sensor samples to create a uniform table. Irrelevant metadata and text annotations were removed so that each entry consists only of the agronomic variables and a single target crop label. For example, entries RSL-001 through RSL-005 in Table \ref{tab:soil_data_records} show specific soil chemical properties aligned with their zoned crops (Corn, Eggplant, etc.), demonstrating how fragmented spreadsheet data was transformed into a structured format for learning.

Data cleaning ensured dataset quality: entries with missing or invalid values (non-numeric, out-of-range) were dropped. Numerical features were bounded to agronomically realistic limits (e.g. soil pH restricted to 3–9) to eliminate outliers. A critical issue was class imbalance. In the raw data, common crops like Corn had thousands of records while rare crops (Ampalaya, Sweet Pepper) had very few. To prevent bias, synthetic oversampling was applied to minority classes. Using a K-Means–based approach, we grouped minority samples and generated perturbed examples around each cluster center. This produced a balanced dataset, as visualized in Figure \ref{fig:data_augmentation}. Before augmentation (red), the class distribution is skewed; after augmentation (green), each of the 10 crops has roughly 2,400 samples, yielding about 8,694 total records (1,191 original + 7,503 synthetic).

\subsection{Feature Engineering}
In addition to the four raw inputs, four derived features were computed to capture agronomic relationships. Two features are ratios: the Phosphorus-to-Potassium ratio $P/K$ and the Phosphorus-to-Organic Matter ratio $P/\mathrm{OM}$. The other two are interaction terms: $\mathrm{OM} \times \mathrm{pH}$ and $K \times \mathrm{pH}$. These encode how nutrient availability changes with soil acidity or organic content. For instance, a low $K \times \mathrm{pH}$ simulates potassium lock-up in acidic soils. The engineered features substantially improved model insight: in the trained LightGBM, the $K \times \mathrm{pH}$ term was the third most important feature, and the phosphorus ratios together contributed nearly $20\%$ of total importance.

These algorithmically derived features, summarized in Table \ref{tab:feature_engineering}, mirror physiological nutrient constraints and bio-availability dynamics that are critical for accurate soil-health assessment.

\subsection{Model Configuration and Training}
The prediction engine is a Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) classifier. LightGBM is an efficient gradient-boosting framework optimized for speed and handling complex feature interactions. It was chosen for its ability to train quickly and run on modest hardware, making it suitable for IoT integration. The eight features (4 raw + 4 engineered) are fed into the classifier, which outputs probabilities for each of the 10 crop classes. Data splits were 70\% training, 15\% validation, 15\% testing (stratified to preserve class ratios). To fine-tune performance, we used Bayesian hyperpa- rameter optimization (Optuna, 50 trials) with 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the training set. We optimized tree depth, number of leaves, learning rate, feature/bagging fractions, and L1/L2 regularization. Early stopping was applied if validation log-loss did not improve for 20 rounds. This process ensured a well-generalized model without overfitting.

\subsection{Performance Metrics and Results}
The final model achieved a test-set accuracy of 83.2\%. Its balanced accuracy (the macro-average recall across classes) was 82.5\%, indicating robust performance on both common and rare crops. The weighted F1-score was 0.831. Figure \ref{fig:classification_report} presents precision, recall, and F1-score per crop. The model performed particularly well on Tobacco ($F_1 \approx 0.96$) and Sorghum, while Eggplant was the most challenging ($F_1 \approx 0.66$), consistent with its confusion with similar crops.

\section{Mobile Application}
The TANIM mobile application provides an integrated platform that allows users, particularly farmers, to access soil data, crop recommendations, and fertilizer guidance directly from their mobile devices. The application is designed with a user-friendly interface that guides users through a step-by-step workflow, from authentication to decision-making and farm management.

The system architecture is designed to serve as the primary gateway for the TANIM framework, beginning with a secure login interface as shown in Figure 1. It acts as a bridge between the physical soil sensors and the user, providing a centralized location for monitoring registered farms and analyzing environmental conditions. By providing a clear organizational structure, the system allows farmers to navigate through different stages of the agricultural cycle with data-driven efficiency.

This included crop-specific fertilizer rates, modes of application protocols, and soil pH preference modeling.

The core fertilizer and crop suitability logic of the system is based entirely on protocols from the Bureau of Soils and Water Management. Validation was performed by ensuring that the programmed logic strictly follows these published standards, thereby eliminating arbitrary decision-making within the recommendation engine. The system recommendations were cross-checked against reference manuals to confirm that every combination of soil sensor data results in agriculturally sound outputs.

A critical aspect of the validation involved converting qualitative color-based results into actionable quantitative limits. In terms of reliability, most predictions exceeded a 70\% confidence threshold, while lower scores effectively flagged ambiguous profiles for further review.


Despite these strengths, misclassifications occurred in crops with overlapping nutrient requirements (e.g., Tomato vs. Eggplant). Furthermore, the model's current focus on soil-only features lacks climatic context such as elevation and seasonality. Future iterations should integrate environmental IoT sensor data and periodic retraining with fresh laboratory results to maintain long-term precision.

This practical approach is evident in their fertilizer application where they combine their own estimates with specific agricultural recommendations. Over the years, the group has successfully cultivated a variety of crops such as corn, cassava, sweet potatoes, and bananas. At present, each participant focuses their time and resources on managing a single farm.

The respondent from the Department of Agriculture (DA) has been serving the institution since 2014, reflecting over a decade of professional experience in the agricultural sector. This extensive tenure suggests a strong familiarity with the department’s programs, policies, and field operations, particularly in supporting farmers and implementing agricultural initiatives.
