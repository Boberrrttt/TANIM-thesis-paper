	\section{Requirements Analysis}
	The system requires the development and implementation of a machine learning component capable of performing soil health prediction, and  crop recommendation. Machine Learning boosting algorithms was employed for these tasks, using both sensor and zoning data. The sensors collects important soil information such as Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K), pH level, moisture, temperature, and salinity. By obtaining the data sources, the system can better understand the relationship between soil condition and the environment. Based on the collected data, the machine learning model classifies the soil quality levels, while crop yield forecasting will leverage an optimized machine learning model by identifying patterns and relationships between soil parameters and environmental factors. To make predictions more accurate, the model uses a metaheuristic-based feature selection that focuses on the most relevant data and removes unnecessary or less useful information. 
	
	After analyzing the soil health parameters and producing crop recommendations, the system generates fertilizer recommendations based on predicted soil nutrient deficiencies, crop nutrient requirements, and established agronomic guidelines. The system determines the appropriate fertilizer type and application rate suited for specific field conditions. To make sure the machine learning model produces reliable results, the dataset was divided into three parts: $70\%$ for training, $15\%$ for validation, and $15\%$ for testing. This ensures that the system learns effectively from the data, maintains accuracy, and avoids overfitting.
	
	The system undergoes proper evaluation using both quantitative and qualitative approaches. Quantitative assessment includes metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score for crop recommendation. RMSE, and MAE was used for consistency ratios and ranking stability for soil health readings. Conducting these metric evaluations is important because they measure how well the machine learning model performs and how reliable its predictions are. By analyzing these values, the researchers can identify the model’s strengths, detect errors, and make adjustments to improve performance before deployment. Cross-validation ensures model robustness across different crop zones. Meanwhile, qualitative evaluation involves focus group discussions and structured interviews with farmers and agricultural technicians in Region 10 to assess usability, reliability, and practical value. Feedback from these sessions will guide further refinement of the system to ensure its effectiveness in real-world agricultural applications.

	\section{Data Collection and Preparation}
	This section describes the procedures and methods used to gather, integrate, and prepare data for the system's operation and machine learning components. It covers the collection of soil, environmental, and geographical data from various sources, as well as the acquisition of historical datasets. Furthermore, it outlines the processes involved in data integration, preprocessing, feature engineering, and dataset preparation to ensure data quality and suitability for analysis. The section also presents how the processed data is stored and managed, and how it flows through the system to support accurate crop and fertilizer recommendations.
	
	\subsection{Soil Data Collection}
	Soil data collection serves as the primary input of the system, providing the foundational information required for crop recommendation and soil health analysis. The main dataset used for training the machine learning model was sourced from the Department of Agriculture (DA) Region 10, comprising verified soil records that include measured parameters such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and soil pH. Because the data originates from an established institutional source, it contributes directly to the accuracy and reliability of the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) model by providing the system with documented patterns between soil conditions and historically suitable crops across the region.
	
	In addition to the DA dataset, the system is designed to support real-time IoT-based soil data collection through sensors connected to an ESP32 microcontroller deployed directly in the field. To ensure comprehensive and spatially representative coverage of each farm plot, three sensor units are positioned across the farmland using a triangular deployment method, with each unit placed at one point of the triangle to capture soil variability across different areas of the same plot. This arrangement distributes the sensing points evenly, reducing the risk of localized readings skewing the overall soil health assessment for the farm. The readings collected from all three units are aggregated and used as the basis for crop recommendation, ensuring that the system reflects the actual soil conditions of the entire plot rather than a single sample point.
	
	In the current implementation, the IoT-collected data is primarily directed toward crop recommendation and real-time soil monitoring rather than model training, though it is also intended to support future dataset expansion as field data accumulates over successive farming cycles. Overall, the DA dataset serves as the foundation for model training, while the triangular IoT deployment framework provides the system with the spatial coverage and real-time sensing capability needed to deliver accurate and field-representative recommendations during active use.
	
	\subsection{Environmental and Weather Data Collection}
	Soil and simple environmental readings from the field are sent to the ML service as one list of numbers. The first values match what the crop model was trained on (organic matter, phosphorus, potassium, and pH). If the device sends more values, those can include things like temperature, moisture, and electrical conductivity. After each prediction, the system saves a pending snapshot that holds the numbers, the full request, an optional farm id, and optional latitude and longitude. The app can then pull that single snapshot instead of combining data from many separate calls.
	
	Weather works differently. The project does not use a weather station on the farm. The backend talks to OpenWeatherMap using lat and long, keeps the API key on the server, and sends the app a clean package with temperature, humidity, pressure, wind, visibility, a short description of the sky, the place name, and a time stamp. The app does not need to hit the weather API constantly, so it can cache results for a short time. Which coordinates are used matters: if the latest ML request already had lat and long, those are used for weather so it matches the same area as the sensor run. If not, the app uses the coordinates stored for that farm. There is also an optional fallback for testing when coordinates are missing. When the farmer has an active saved session, the screen shows the saved soil data from the server for consistency. When there is no active session, the app checks often for new pending readings so the numbers stay up to date.
	
	This setup is intentional. Soil measurements and internet weather are not the same thing, but showing both together gives a clearer picture. Putting the weather API on the backend hides the key and keeps responses in one standard format. Using one feature list and one pending snapshot keeps the model, the server, and the app on the same page and avoids extra work in the mobile client.
	
	
	\subsection{Zoning Data Collection}
	Geographic representation matters so farm records are not just names on a list but real places on the ground. The system needs to know where farming happens, roughly how big the holding is, and stable coordinates that other tools can use, such as weather APIs and machine learning workflows. Right now, location is stored mostly as a point: each farm can have latitude and longitude, plus an optional text field for things like barangay or street, and a field for how large the farm is said to be. Admins and users enter this through the web when they create or edit a farm. The API can also update coordinates when a session or a sync from the model layer brings in new positions, so people do not have to fix the map by hand as often.
	
	In this study, the term “zoning” means organizing farms in space, not official land-use zones drawn as polygons on a cadastral map. The admin GIS Mapping screen shows farms on a Leaflet map with OpenStreetMap tiles. Farms with real latitude and longitude show up as pins. Farms without coordinates stay off the map until someone adds GPS data, and the UI warns you if registered farms still have no usable GPS. If there is more than one farm, the map zooms to fit all the pins. The default map center fits the Philippines so the first view is not empty. Clicking a pin shows extra details like soil type and area so you can relate the site to soil conditions. Soil tests are linked to the same farm id, so lab or field results line up with that place in the dashboard

	\subsection{Data Integration}
	The primary objective of the data integration phase is to unify multiple, heterogeneous data streams into a single, cohesive dataset suitable for advanced computational analysis and machine learning training. By centralizing disparate information into a uniform structure, the system ensures that the predictive models can process environmental, and historical variables simultaneously. This integration is essential for creating a holistic representation of the agricultural environment, bridging the gap between local sensor observations and broader regional contexts.
	
	As illustrated in the integration pipeline in Figure \ref{fig:integration_pipeline}, the system aggregates data from external sources. The pipeline depicts the convergence of LoRa-based sensor data, REST-based weather APIs, and batch-imported historical records into a unified dataset. Real-time data is captured via an ESP32 IoT sensor node, which transmits continuous readings of Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K), pH levels, soil moisture, salinity, and temperature. These hardware-derived metrics are combined with external data streams, including localized weather updates retrieved through HTTP/REST APIs and the validated Consolidated Soil Info (RSL) 2023--2025 historical dataset. This multi-source approach allows the framework to factor in both immediate soil conditions and long-term agricultural trends.

	\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
	The primary objective of data preprocessing within this framework is to systematically refine and clean the integrated dataset, thereby reducing algorithmic bias and ensuring high accuracy during the subsequent machine learning training phase. Given that the machine learning models rely on the structural integrity of the input data to generate reliable crop and fertilizer recommendations, this phase acts as a critical filter. It transforms raw, heterogeneous data into a standardized format that facilitates efficient mathematical computation and pattern recognition. 
	
	Figure \ref{fig:SequentialPreprocessingPipeline} illustrates the sequential pipeline of the system. The initial stage involves rigorous data cleaning to address missing or empty values, these gaps are handled based on the statistical variation of the feature. Depending on the feature's distribution, these gaps are addressed through mathematical substitution, such as applying the feature mean or using linear interpolation, or through the systematic removal of records showing excessive missing data to maintain dataset integrity.

	Following the initial cleaning, noise and outlier handling mechanisms are applied to filter unusual data points generated by temporary sensor glitches or transmission errors. This process employs statistical limits, specifically Z-score thresholding, to identify and discard extreme values that could otherwise skew the decision boundaries of the model. The impact of this process is evident in Figure 3, where the "Before Preprocessing" box plots indicate several extreme outliers in the Nitrogen (N) and Phosphorus (P) features. By removing these points, the system ensures that the training process is grounded in realistic agricultural conditions rather than technical anomalies. 
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\caption{Comparison of Soil Parameter Distributions Before and After Preprocessing}
		\label{fig:BoxplotComparison}
		\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/BoxplotComparison.pdf}
	\end{figure}
	
	Figure \ref{fig:BoxplotComparison} shows that soil parameters such as potassium and nitrogen exist on scales significantly larger than parameters such as temperature and moisture, highlighting the need for normalization and scaling techniques to be applied to the input features prior to model training. Without scaling, features with larger raw magnitudes would contribute more heavily to the model's calculations simply due to their numerical range rather than their actual predictive importance, which would introduce bias into the learned weights and reduce overall model performance.
	To address this, a standard scaler was applied to all continuous numerical features, transforming each parameter into a common scale where the mean is zero and the standard deviation is one. This process, known as z-score standardization, recalculates each feature value as the number of standard deviations it falls from the mean of that feature across the training set. The transformation ensures that all input features contribute to the model on an equal numerical footing, allowing the algorithm to assign weights based on the actual relationship between each feature and the target variable rather than on the raw magnitude of the input. This standardization is essential for the stability and convergence of the machine learning model, particularly in algorithms that are sensitive to the scale of input data.
	
	Algorithmic encoding is performed to translate categorical variables into numerical representations. Since the system’s backend requires mathematical inputs for computation, non-numeric data, most notably the targeted crop types are transformed using techniques such as label encoding or one-hot encoding. This process ensures that categorical labels are represented as distinct mathematical entities, allowing the model to process variety-specific data without assuming an incorrect ordinal relationship between different crops. 
	
	The preprocessing pipeline concludes with comprehensive programmatic consistency checks to definitively verify the structural integrity and uniformity of the cleaned dataset. This final audit ensures that all processed records adhere to the predefined schema and that no data corruption occurred during the transformation stages. Once verified, the dataset is formally transitioned into the primary database, serving as a high-fidelity reference for the system’s recommendation engine. 
	
	\subsection{Feature Engineering}
	The primary objective of the feature engineering phase was to augment the predictive performance of the machine learning models by providing more nuanced and expressive data representations. Rather than relying exclusively on the raw soil properties captured through the sensor network and historical datasets, this phase involved mathematically deriving new variables that encode established agronomic relationships. By transforming raw measurements into high-order features, the system enables the algorithms to capture complex, non-linear dependencies between soil chemistry and plant health that are not explicitly present in the initial input vectors.
	
	Two distinct categories of derived features were constructed for the engineered dataset. The first category comprised ratio-based features designed to quantify the balance between specific nutrients. The Phosphorus-to-Potassium ratio ($P/K$) was calculated to define the internal nutrient equilibrium between these two primary macronutrients, which serves as a critical fertility indicator in tropical soil profiles. Additionally, the Phosphorus-to-Organic Matter ratio ($P/OM$) was computed to express Phosphorus availability relative to the soil's organic composition, reflecting the established agronomic dependency of Phosphorus mobility on organic matter levels.
	
	The second category consisted of interaction features, derived through multiplicative operations to encode conditional relationships. An Organic Matter $\times$ pH interaction term was generated to represent the principle that organic matter decomposition and subsequent nutrient release are strongly regulated by soil acidity. Similarly, a Potassium $\times$ pH interaction term was derived to capture the pH-dependent nature of Potassium uptake by root systems. These interaction terms allowed the model to learn complex interdependencies without requiring manual thresholding or excessively deep neural architectures.
	
	The explicit encoding of these soil-crop relationships reduced the computational complexity required for the model to discover such patterns independently. By providing calculated nutrient ratios and pH-dependent interaction terms, the algorithm leveraged domain-specific knowledge directly, resulting in faster convergence during the training phase and improved generalization when processing unseen soil conditions. This engineering process successfully expanded the dataset from the four original raw variables to a comprehensive set of eight predictive features.

	\subsection{Data Processing Workflow}
	The TANIM system employs a multi-stage data pipeline designed to transform raw environmental signals into actionable agricultural intelligence. This workflow integrates physical sensing, cloud-based data management, and advanced machine learning analytics to provide decision-support tools for both farmers and regional agricultural authorities. 
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\caption{TANIM Data Processing Workflow Pipeline}

		\label{fig:DataProcessingWorkflow}
	\end{figure}
	
	Figure \ref{fig:DataProcessingWorkflow} illustrates the comprehensive data flow, from initial acquisition to end-user dissemination, following a logical progression through hardware interfacing, cloud integration, and predictive modeling. The workflow demonstrates the transition from raw sensor acquisition to the generation of agricultural insights.
	
	The primary stage of the workflow involves the high-fidelity collection of soil parameters at the physical layer. The system utilizes a multi-parameter soil sensor to monitor Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K), pH, moisture, salinity, and temperature, all of which are interfaced with an ESP32 microcontroller. To ensure data integrity during local transmission, a MAX485 module is employed to convert RS-485 differential signals to TTL-compatible logic for the microcontroller. These data packets are subsequently encapsulated and transmitted via the LoRa (Long Range) protocol to a central gateway. This modulation technique is specifically selected to ensure reliable connectivity in topographically challenging or remote agricultural zones where traditional cellular infrastructure is often insufficient.
	
	Upon receipt at the LoRa gateway, the data is forwarded to a Cloud database for centralized management and refinement. During this phase, the system performs rigorous data cleaning by executing filtering algorithms to mitigate electrical noise and remove statistical outliers from the raw sensor readings. Furthermore, a data fusion process contextualizes the real-time sensor data by integrating it with secondary datasets. These include meteorological information, such as precipitation and humidity acquired via external APIs, and geospatial zoning maps provided by the Department of Agriculture (DA) Region 10.
	
	The analytical core of the system leverages a LightGBM algorithm, a gradient boosting framework utilized for its high efficiency and superior performance with tabular agricultural datasets. The process begins with metaheuristic-based feature selection to identify the most significant soil and climatic variables, thereby reducing dimensionality and improving model generalization. The predictive engine then performs multi-task analytics, which include classifying soil health status based on established agronomic thresholds, forecasting crop suitability to optimize regional yield performance, and generating site-specific fertilizer recommendations. The fertilizer recommendation module evaluates the real-time NPK levels and pH readings against crop-specific nutrient requirements, classifying each macronutrient as deficient, adequate, or excessive. Based on these classifications and the soil's pH-dependent nutrient availability profile, the system prescribes targeted fertilizer application rates and formulations, ensuring that nutrient management strategies are both agronomically sound and resource-efficient.
	
	
	\section{Machine Learning Methodology}
	
	This section presents the machine learning methodology employed in the TANIM system, detailing the model architecture, data balancing technique, hyperparameter optimization strategy, training and validation procedures, and the performance metrics used to evaluate the system's crop recommendation capability.
	
	\subsection{LightGBM Architecture}
	The TANIM project used a LightGBM model because it processes data quickly and accurately sorts soil information into different crop categories. Once the dataset is balanced, the cleaned and augmented soil records are passed through a feature engineering step where four additional interaction variables are calculated from the original soil readings. These include ratios and combinations such as the relationship between potassium and pH, which capture how nutrients interact with each other in real soil conditions. The final set of eight features is then used to train the LightGBM model, which builds a series of decision trees that progressively learn to distinguish the correct crop recommendation based on the soil profile provided.
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\caption{LightGBM Model Architecture used in the TANIM system}
		\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/LightGBMModel.pdf}
		\label{fig:lightgbm}
	\end{figure}
	
	The integration of LightGBM with a balanced dataset enhances classification accuracy and ensures fair representation of all soil categories. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:lightgbm}, the LightGBM architecture uses an ensemble of decision trees that aggregate predictions to produce final crop classifications. This structure allows the system to efficiently process soil parameters and generate accurate crop recommendations, ultimately helping improve harvest yields and optimize resource utilization.
	
	\subsection{K-Means SMOTE}
	Since regional soil data often has uneven amounts of records for various crops, the system applies K-Means SMOTE to address this imbalance before any training begins. K-Means SMOTE works by first grouping the existing minority crop samples into natural clusters based on their soil properties. It then generates additional synthetic soil samples around each cluster center by introducing small and controlled variations to the existing values. This ensures that the new samples closely resemble realistic soil conditions rather than being random or artificial data.

	\subsection{Hyperparameter Optimization}
	
	To further enhance model performance and generalization capability, the training process incorporated Optuna, an advanced hyperparameter optimization framework, to systematically identify the most optimal configuration for the machine learning model. A total of 50 optimization trials were executed, where each trial explored different combinations of hyperparameters such as learning rate, tree depth, number of estimators, and leaf configurations. This automated search process enabled the selection of parameter values that maximized predictive performance while minimizing overfitting.
	
	Simultaneously, the prepared dataset composed of the eight engineered soil and environmental features was clearly separated into input features and target variables. The framework addressed two predictive objectives: crop recommendation was formulated as a multiclass classification problem, while fertilizer recommendation outputs were treated as quantitative prediction variables.
	
	To ensure robustness and reliability during optimization, each Optuna trial employed 5-Fold Stratified Cross-Validation. In this procedure, the training dataset was divided into five equally distributed subsets while preserving the proportional distribution of crop classes across each fold. During every iteration, four subsets were used for training and one subset was reserved for validation, rotating sequentially until all folds had been evaluated. Because this validation strategy was repeated across all 50 optimization trials, the system performed a total of 250 internal model evaluations. This comprehensive validation process reduced the likelihood of bias caused by a single favorable data split and ensured that the final model configuration demonstrated strong generalization capability across varying agricultural conditions.

	\subsection{System Architecture}
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\caption{System Architecture}
		\label{fig:SystemArchitecture}
		\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/finalSYSARCH.pdf}
	\end{figure}
	
	Figure \ref{fig:SystemArchitecture} shows the system architecture for the Soil Health Monitoring, Fertilizer, and Crop Recommendation System (TANIM). The system flows from hardware deployed in the field, through a LoRa wireless link and a local gateway, into the cloud for storage and machine learning, and finally out to a web application for agricultural authorities and a mobile app for farmers.
	
	The sensing unit is packaged as a single IoT device deployed in the field. A solar panel feeds into a Solar Panel Manager, which handles charging and power regulation so the device can operate continuously in remote areas far from the power grid. Inside the device, an ESP32 microcontroller reads the sensors, performs basic processing on the readings, and builds the data packets to be transmitted.
	
	A soil sensor measures parameters such as NPK, pH, and moisture. The sensor communicates over an industrial serial protocol compatible with RS-485, which is well-suited for long cable runs in electrically noisy environments. A TTL MAX485 module converts this signal into a standard UART interface that the ESP32 can read directly, enabling reliable communication without adding unnecessary complexity to the firmware.
	
	The device also includes a GPS module. The ESP32 reads the GPS data and bundles location coordinates together with the soil readings before transmission. This allows the cloud backend and the TANIM Web App to display data geographically, showing maps and area-based trends rather than just a plain list of sensor values. Once the ESP32 has prepared the data, it transmits the packet through a LoRa Module, which is designed for low power consumption and long-range communication, making it well-suited for agricultural and rural deployments.
	
	A LoRa Receiver listens for incoming packets from one or more field nodes and is paired with a second ESP32 that serves as the gateway. This gateway ESP32 receives the LoRa traffic, validates the data, buffers or reformats packets as needed, and forwards everything upstream to the cloud. The forwarded payload includes both soil readings and GPS location.
	
	After leaving the gateway, the data enters the Supabase and Render cloud services, then it is routed into two parallel paths. One path leads to a Machine Learning model, which processes incoming data alongside historical records to identify patterns in soil conditions, detect potential issues such as nutrient deficiencies or moisture stress, and generate fertilizer suggestions, crop recommendations, and actionable soil health insights. The other path leads to Supabase, which serves as the primary backend for the system. Supabase manages the database for storing sensor history, handles user authentication and role-based access control, and exposes APIs for querying sensor records, user profiles, and data visibility rules, particularly for authority-level users.
	
	The TANIM Web App is designed for agricultural authorities, drawing on Supabase for underlying data access and presenting views such as dashboards and maps, supplemented by machine learning outputs where applicable. The TANIM Mobile App is designed for farmers, presenting information in a more accessible format covering soil insights, crop and fertilizer recommendations, and alerts. Both applications use Supabase for secure, authenticated access to stored data and connect to the machine learning service when model-based recommendations are needed.

	The Users entity consolidates both administrative and farmer roles into a single table to avoid redundancy and improve system maintainability. It stores unique identification and authentication credentials, along with a role attribute that distinguishes between administrative users and farmers. This unified design simplifies access control while maintaining clear role-based separation within the system. 	The farmer functionality is also represented within the same Users entity, where individual user profiles include detailed information such as full names, contact numbers, and secure login credentials. Through a one-to-many relationship, a single user classified as a farmer can register and manage multiple agricultural plots stored in the Farm entity.
	
	The Farm entity stores essential metadata for each registered agricultural plot, including the farm name, land area measurement, and precise geographic coordinates to support spatial mapping and location-based tracking. Each farm is associated with one farmer through a foreign key and acts as the central reference point connecting soil measurements and farming activity records.

It is linked to the Farm entity through a one-to-many relationship, allowing each farm to accumulate multiple soil health test records over time. Each record captures macronutrient levels, including nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, together with pH, salinity, temperature, and moisture readings. Created and updated timestamps are also maintained to preserve a verifiable history of how soil conditions evolve for each agricultural plot.
	
	The Farming Session entity manages operational data and model-driven insights by functioning as a log of field activity for each planting cycle. It links a specific farmer to a specific farm for the duration of a session and archives soil snapshots, fertilizer recommendations, and machine learning-generated crop probability outputs within each session record. The entity also captures the farmer’s final crop selection and tracks each cycle through start, end, and creation timestamps, maintaining a complete historical archive of soil conditions and decision-making across every session.
	
	The Crop Fertilizer Specifications entity provides a reference layer of fertilizer product data used to interpret soil results and support recommendation logic. Each specification row is identified by a primary key and stores attributes such as type, grade, and label, along with supplied nutrient values for nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in the fields n\_supplied, p\_supplied, and k\_supplied. A created\_at timestamp is also maintained for each record. As a standalone reference relation in the schema, this entity normalizes fertilizer parameters so they can be managed and updated independently from farm, soil test, and farming session data.
	
	Operational data and model-driven insights are managed within the Farming Session entity, which functions as a log of field activity for each planting cycle. This table links a specific farmer to a specific farm for the duration of a session. This design enables the system to maintain a historical archive of soil health conditions and decision-making for every visit or cycle. 	Figure \ref{fig:HardwareFlowchart} illustrates the sequential workflow of the hardware components in the TANIM IoT device. The process begins with two input sources that feed into the Microcontroller Node, which is the Soil Health Sensor and the GPS Module.
	
	The Soil Health Sensor captures the core environmental parameters required for soil analysis, specifically moisture, temperature, pH, salinity, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. The raw readings from this sensor are passed through a Transceiver, which facilitates the communication interface between the sensor and the Microcontroller Node, converting the sensor output into a usable Sensor Data signal that the microcontroller can receive and process.
	
	Simultaneously, the GPS Module acquires satellite signals to determine the precise geographic position of the device. The module outputs longitude and latitude coordinates, which are fed directly into the Microcontroller Node alongside the sensor data. This ensures that every soil reading is paired with its corresponding geographic location before any further processing takes place.
	
	The Microcontroller Node serves as the central processing unit at this stage of the pipeline. It receives both the sensor data from the transceiver and the location coordinates from the GPS module, then applies filtering and calibration routines to improve data accuracy and remove noise or erroneous readings. The result is a set of filtered and calibrated data that is ready for transmission.
	
	The filtered and calibrated data is then passed to the Transmitter, which encapsulates the information into structured data packets and transmits them wirelessly over a long-range communication link. A Receiver on the other end captures these incoming packets and forwards the averaged, filtered, and calibrated data to the Microcontroller Master. The Microcontroller Master acts as the gateway-side processing unit, handling the received data and preparing it for upstream delivery to the cloud infrastructure.
	
	Taken as a whole, the block diagram represents a strictly sequential hardware pipeline in which each stage depends on the successful completion of the previous one. The Microcontroller Node coordinates sensor data acquisition, GPS integration, and initial data processing, while the Transceiver, Transmitter, and Receiver modules ensure that data is reliably communicated from the field device to the gateway. The Microcontroller Master then consolidates the incoming data before it is forwarded to the rest of the system.

	\section{Implementation}
	The Implementation phase involves developing the software and hardware components. Each function was ensured to function according to the design specifications.This phase intends to transform the conceptual design into a working prototype that can perform data acquisition, processing, and output for TANIM.
	
	\subsection{Hardware Implementation}
	The hardware implementation of the TANIM system is centered on an IoT-based device that collects soil data and transmits it to the cloud for processing. The system is built around the ESP32 microcontroller, selected for its integrated Wi-Fi and Bluetooth connectivity, low power consumption, and compatibility with multiple sensor modules, making it suitable for continuous field monitoring in remote agricultural environments. A 7-in-1 soil sensor is utilized to measure key parameters including pH, NPK, moisture, temperature, and electrical conductivity, with communication established via RS-485 using the Modbus RTU protocol, allowing the ESP32 to periodically request and parse sensor data for real-time analysis.
	
	To improve data reliability under field conditions, a two-stage filtering pipeline is implemented in the firmware. A sliding median filter with a window size of nine samples is first applied to remove outliers and transient noise, followed by an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) filter with a smoothing factor of $\alpha = 0.25$ to ensure temporal stability and reduce high-frequency fluctuations. A mandatory five-minute soak period is also enforced upon system startup to allow sensor stabilization, during which data collection and filtering continue but transmission is suppressed to prevent inaccurate readings.
	
	A GPS module provides geolocation data for mapping soil readings to specific field coordinates, with continuous non-blocking parsing to ensure that location acquisition does not interfere with sensor sampling and transmission processes. For long-range communication, a LoRa module is utilized, implementing a Time Division Multiple Access (TDMA) scheme where each node transmits within a predefined time slot to prevent packet collisions. Transmission reliability is ensured through a stop-and-wait acknowledgment mechanism, wherein each packet awaits an ACK within a 1.5-second window and retries up to three times if no confirmation is received. Additionally, an I2C LCD is integrated for on-site monitoring, displaying real-time sensor readings and system status through cyclic information screens.
	
	A separate ESP32-based gateway acts as the receiver, collecting data from multiple nodes and forwarding it to the cloud. The gateway acknowledges received packets, validates node identities, and organizes incoming data into per-minute buffers with a 30-second collection window, requiring at least two nodes before processing proceeds while discarding duplicate packets to prevent redundancy. Once the collection condition is satisfied, the gateway computes average values for each parameter, selects valid GPS coordinates, and formats the aggregated data into a JSON payload, which is transmitted via HTTP POST to the cloud-based prediction API for crop and fertilizer recommendation.
	
	\subsection{Software Implementation}
	The software implementation for TANIM is designed to provide an efficient, reliable, and user-friendly platform that caters to the needs of both the Farmer and the Department of Agriculture. To ensure optimal accessibility and performance, the frontend for farmers is developed using React Native, a framework well-suited for creating responsive and cross-platform mobile applications. The mobile application also features offline data handling, allowing farmers to access previously synced soil and crop data even without an internet connection. Any new inputs or updates made while offline are temporarily stored locally on the device and automatically synchronized with the cloud once connectivity is restored. This ensures uninterrupted functionality in rural areas with limited network coverage.
	
	Meanwhile, the frontend for the Department of Agriculture is implemented using React.js, providing a seamless and interactive web application experience. The web application integrates advanced analytics and visualization tools, enabling agricultural authorities to interpret soil data, monitor farm conditions, and analyze regional agricultural trends through interactive charts, dashboards, and geospatial maps. These insights support data-driven planning, resource allocation, and policy formulation.
	
	Both user interfaces interact with a shared backend developed in Flask, which centralizes data management and ensures consistent functionality across platforms. This architecture enables efficient communication between the mobile and web applications while supporting scalability, maintainability, and ease of integration with additional features or services in the future.
	
	\subsection{Data Storage and Management}
	The architecture of the proposed system necessitates a centralized and reliable repository to ensure data consistency across administrative and end-user platforms. This repository facilitates the management of diverse entities, including farmer and administrator accounts, farm records encompassing geographic coordinates and land area, active farming sessions, and soil test readings. Furthermore, the database stores the outputs of the machine learning engine, specifically crop and fertilizer recommendations, alongside system telemetry and device status updates. While machine learning models are maintained as distinct training artifacts, all operational and longitudinal data are housed within the database to ensure that the web dashboard, mobile application, and backend services utilize a unified source of truth.
	
	The system utilizes a standard relational database management system, specifically PostgreSQL, which is deployed via a managed cloud service to ensure high availability and automated maintenance. The backend Application Programming Interface (API) establishes a secure connection to the database, organizing data into structured tables for users, farms, sessions, soil tests, and recommendations. To optimize schema complexity, richer nested data structures are stored using structured fields within the relational environment, reducing the need for an excessive number of auxiliary tables while maintaining query efficiency. The mobile application interacts with this layer through standard HTTP requests, while the web application leverages a Representational State Transfer (REST) layer provided by the hosting service to streamline Create, Read, Update, and Delete (CRUD) operations without schema duplication.
	
	Security and scalability are integral to the data management strategy of the system. User authentication is secured through strong cryptographic hashing for passwords, while session management is handled via a dual-token system consisting of short-lived access tokens and longer-lived refresh tokens. These tokens are stored within secure on-device storage on the mobile platform and verified against secrets maintained in the server configuration. Furthermore, the hosting environment implements Row-Level Security (RLS) policies to ensure that users are restricted to accessing only their authorized data subsets. To accommodate increasing traffic and data volume, the system employs connection pooling, stateless API instances capable of horizontal scaling behind a load balancer, and paginated data retrieval to minimize latency and server overhead. Consistent data integrity is maintained through automated backup protocols managed by the database provider, ensuring recovery in the event of system failure.

	\subsection{Hardware Testing and Calibration}
	The soil sensor pH parameter was submitted to the Department of Science and Technology (DOST) for formal calibration using three certified buffer solutions at pH 4.0, 7.0, and 10.0. The raw sensor readings recorded at each reference point were 4.8, 5.7, and 9.0, respectively.

	Table \ref{tab:calibration} presents the raw sensor output against the known buffer values and the corresponding error at each point. The error pattern observed across the three reference points was non-uniform in both direction and magnitude. At pH 4.0, the sensor over-read by 0.8 units, producing a raw value higher than the true reference. At pH 7.0 and pH 10.0, the sensor under-read by 1.3 units in both cases, returning values lower than the certified buffer. This inconsistency across the measurement range indicates a non-linear response characteristic in the sensor, meaning the deviation does not follow a consistent direction or magnitude as pH increases. Because the error shifts from positive to negative and varies in size, a single constant offset applied uniformly across the range would not be sufficient to bring all three points into acceptable agreement with their reference values. Three calibration approaches were therefore evaluated and compared to determine the most appropriate correction method: a single constant offset, linear regression, and a three-point quadratic fit.
	
	For the single constant offset approach, a uniform correction of $+0.5$ pH units was derived by averaging the three required corrections, calculated as
	\[
	\frac{-0.8 + 1.3 + 1.3}{3} = +0.5.
	\]
	This correction was applied uniformly to all raw sensor readings regardless of the pH range.

	As shown in Table \ref{tab:corrected}, the corrected values at pH 4.0, 7.0, and 10.0 were 5.30, 6.20, and 9.50, respectively, resulting in residual errors of +1.30, -0.80, and -0.50. While this method is simple to implement, the residual errors remain relatively large, particularly at the lower end of the range, confirming that a single constant offset is insufficient to correct for the non-linear behavior observed in the sensor response. This outcome further motivated the evaluation of linear regression and a three-point quadratic fit as alternative correction approaches capable of better accounting for the varying error across the pH range.
	
	To validate the nutrient measurements for nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, the system employed a Soil Test Kit (STK) method as a practical cross-reference against established agronomic thresholds. Since high-precision laboratory instrumentation was not available for direct quantitative comparison, the STK approach provided a qualitative verification by categorizing nutrient levels into Low, Medium, and High based on predefined concentration ranges. Sensor readings were then evaluated against these categories to determine whether both methods produced consistent classifications for the same soil samples.
	
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\caption{Soil Test Kit Quantitative Values for Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium, and pH}
		\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/QuantiValuesSTK.pdf}
		\label{fig:STKValues}
	\end{figure}
	
	Figure \ref{fig:STKValues} shows the quantitative thresholds used for STK classification. 
	For nitrogen, expressed as percent organic matter (\% OM), Low is at or below 1.7\%, 
	Medium ranges from above 1.7\% to 3.0\%, and High exceeds 3.0\%. For phosphorus, 
	the applicable test method depends on soil pH. In acidic to neutral soils (pH $<$ 7.3), 
	the Bray-1 method is used, where Low is at or below 10~ppm, Medium ranges from above 
	10 to 20~ppm, and High exceeds 20~ppm. In alkaline soils (pH $\geq$ 7.3), the Olsen 
	method applies, where Low is at or below 7~ppm, Medium ranges from above 7 to 25~ppm, 
	and High exceeds 25~ppm. For potassium, Low is at or below 117~ppm, Medium ranges 
	from above 117 to 235~ppm, and High exceeds 235~ppm. For soil pH, Low corresponds 
	to values below 6.6 indicating acidic conditions, Medium ranges from 6.6 to 7.0 
	indicating neutral conditions, and High exceeds 7.0 indicating alkaline conditions.
	
	A sensor reading was considered consistent with the STK result if it fell within the 
	same categorical range (Low, Medium, or High) as defined by these thresholds. This 
	threshold-based comparison confirms whether the calibrated sensor outputs align with 
	agronomic interpretations used in standard soil testing practices.
	
	To further evaluate calibration performance, the corrected sensor outputs were compared 
	against reference values using two statistical metrics: Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) 
	and Mean Absolute Error (MAE). RMSE assigns greater weight to larger deviations due 
	to squaring, while MAE treats all deviations uniformly.
	
	\[
	RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{Y}_i - Y_i)^2}
	\]
	
	\[
	MAE = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left| \hat{Y}_i - Y_i \right|
	\]
	
	where $n$ is the number of calibration points, $\hat{Y}_i$ is the calibrated sensor 
	output, and $Y_i$ is the reference value. Together, these metrics provide a quantitative 
	measure of calibration accuracy across all nutrient parameters.
