\section{Requirements Analysis}
The system requires the development and implementation of a machine learning component capable of performing soil health prediction, and  crop recommendation. Machine Learning boosting algorithms will be employed for these tasks, using both sensor and zoning data. The sensors will collect important soil information such as Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K), pH level, moisture, temperature, and salinity. At the same time, zoning data from the Department of Agriculture will provide information about the type of land, elevation, and climate conditions of the area. By combining these two data sources, the system can better understand the relationship between soil condition and the environment. Based on the collected data, the machine learning model will classify soil quality levels, while crop yield forecasting will leverage an optimized machine learning model by identifying patterns and relationships between soil parameters and environmental factors. To make predictions more accurate, the model will use a metaheuristic-based feature selection that focuses on the most relevant data and removes unnecessary or less useful information. 
	
After analyzing the soil health parameters and producing crop recommendations, the system will generate fertilizer recommendations based on predicted soil nutrient deficiencies, crop nutrient requirements, and established agronomic guidelines. The system will determine the appropriate fertilizer type and application rate suited for specific field conditions. To make sure the machine learning model produces reliable results, the dataset will be divided into three parts: $70\%$ for training, $15\%$ for validation, and $15\%$ for testing. This ensures that the system learns effectively from the data, maintains accuracy, and avoids overfitting.
	
The system will undergo proper evaluation using both quantitative and qualitative approaches. Quantitative assessment will include metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score for soil health prediction. R², RMSE, and MAE for crop yield forecasting and consistency ratios and ranking stability indices for crop recommendation. Conducting these metric evaluations is important because they measure how well the machine learning model performs and how reliable its predictions are. By analyzing these values, the researchers can identify the model’s strengths, detect errors, and make adjustments to improve performance before deployment. Cross-validation will ensure model robustness across different crop zones.Meanwhile, qualitative evaluation will involve focus group discussions and structured interviews with farmers and agricultural technicians in Region 10 to assess usability, reliability, and practical value. Feedback from these sessions will guide further refinement of the system to ensure its effectiveness in real-world agricultural applications.

\section{Data Collection and Processing}
	This section describes the procedures and methods used to gather, integrate, and prepare data for the system's operation and machine learning components. It covers the collection of soil, environmental, and geographical data from various sources, as well as the acquisition of historical datasets. Furthermore, it outlines the processes involved in data integration, preprocessing, feature engineering, and dataset preparation to ensure data quality and suitability for analysis. The section also presents how the processed data is stored and managed, and how it flows through the system to support accurate crop and fertilizer recommendations.
	
\subsection{Soil Data Collection}
Soil data collection serves as the primary input of the system, providing essential information for crop recommendation and soil health analysis. In this study, soil data were collected through a combination of IoT-based sensors and secondary data from the Department of Agriculture (DA) Region 10 to ensure both real-time and historical coverage. 

The IoT sensing unit, integrated with an ESP32 microcontroller, measures key soil parameters such as nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), soil pH, moisture, temperature, and salinity, which are critical in determining soil fertility and crop suitability. Data collection follows a periodic sampling approach, where readings are captured at set intervals and stored digitally for consistency and analysis. 

To enhance data reliability and coverage, historical soil datasets and laboratory records from DA Region 10 were also incorporated. This combination of real-time sensor data and secondary sources ensures a more comprehensive and representative dataset for subsequent processing and machine learning tasks. 

Overall, the collected soil data form the foundation of the system, enabling accurate analysis, crop prediction, and fertilizer recommendation. 

\subsection{Environmental and Weather Data Collection}
Soil and simple environmental readings from the field are sent to the ML service as one list of numbers. The first values match what the crop model was trained on (organic matter, phosphorus, potassium, and pH). If the device sends more values, those can include things like temperature, moisture, and electrical conductivity. After each prediction, the system saves a pending snapshot that holds the numbers, the full request, an optional farm id, and optional latitude and longitude. The app can then pull that single snapshot instead of combining data from many separate calls.

Weather works differently. The project does not use a weather station on the farm. The backend talks to OpenWeatherMap using lat and long, keeps the API key on the server, and sends the app a clean package with temperature, humidity, pressure, wind, visibility, a short description of the sky, the place name, and a time stamp. The app does not need to hit the weather API constantly, so it can cache results for a short time. Which coordinates are used matters: if the latest ML request already had lat and long, those are used for weather so it matches the same area as the sensor run. If not, the app uses the coordinates stored for that farm. There is also an optional fallback for testing when coordinates are missing. When the farmer has an active saved session, the screen shows the saved soil data from the server for consistency. When there is no active session, the app checks often for new pending readings so the numbers stay up to date.

This setup is intentional. Soil measurements and internet weather are not the same thing, but showing both together gives a clearer picture. Putting the weather API on the backend hides the key and keeps responses in one standard format. Using one feature list and one pending snapshot keeps the model, the server, and the app on the same page and avoids extra work in the mobile client.


\subsection{Zoning and Geographical Data Collection}
Geographic representation matters so farm records are not just names on a list but real places on the ground. The system needs to know where farming happens, roughly how big the holding is, and stable coordinates that other tools can use, such as weather APIs and machine learning workflows. Right now, location is stored mostly as a point: each farm can have latitude and longitude, plus an optional text field for things like barangay or street, and a field for how large the farm is said to be. Admins and users enter this through the web when they create or edit a farm. The API can also update coordinates when a session or a sync from the model layer brings in new positions, so people do not have to fix the map by hand as often.

In this study, the term “zoning” means organizing farms in space, not official land-use zones drawn as polygons on a cadastral map. The admin GIS Mapping screen shows farms on a Leaflet map with OpenStreetMap tiles. Farms with real latitude and longitude show up as pins. Farms without coordinates stay off the map until someone adds GPS data, and the UI warns you if registered farms still have no usable GPS. If there is more than one farm, the map zooms to fit all the pins. The default map center fits the Philippines so the first view is not empty. Clicking a pin shows extra details like soil type and area so you can relate the site to soil conditions. Soil tests are linked to the same farm id, so lab or field results line up with that place in the dashboard

By centralizing disparate information into a uniform structure, the system ensures that the predictive models can process environmental, historical, and real-time variables simultaneously. As illustrated in the integration pipeline in \textbf{Figure \ref{fig:integration_pipeline}}, the system aggregates data from both real-time and external sources. These hardware-derived metrics are combined with external data streams, including localized weather updates retrieved through HTTP/REST APIs and the validated Consolidated Soil Info (RSL) 2023--2025 historical dataset. This multi-source approach allows the framework to factor in both immediate soil conditions and long-term agricultural trends.

To ensure contextual accuracy, the system employs rigorous temporal and spatial synchronization. Data ingestion occurs through different protocols depending on the source: LoRa transmission for hardware sensors, RESTful requests for weather data, and batch imports for historical records. Once ingested, these data points are programmatically aligned based on matching timestamps and geographic coordinates. This synchronization forms a complete environmental snapshot, ensuring that a specific sensor reading is correctly paired with the weather conditions and historical soil health data relevant to that exact time and location.

During the aggregation process, structural inconsistencies and varying data formats are resolved through a dedicated format standardization and unit conversion layer. Because different sources often utilize varying measurement scales, the system applies strict conversion rules to ensure uniformity. The culmination of this pipeline is the creation of a Unified Target Dataset. This centralized repository effectively bridges real-time sensor baselines with extensive historical context and atmospheric data. By outputting a standardized, synchronized matrix of information, the integration phase provides the high-fidelity data foundation necessary for the subsequent preprocessing and machine learning stages of the system.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
The primary objective of data preprocessing within this framework is to systematically refine and clean the integrated dataset, thereby reducing algorithmic bias and ensuring high accuracy during the subsequent machine learning training phase. Given that the machine learning models rely on the structural integrity of the input data to generate reliable crop and fertilizer recommendations, this phase acts as a critical filter. It transforms raw, heterogeneous data into a standardized format that facilitates efficient mathematical computation and pattern recognition. 

The initial stage involves rigorous data cleaning to address missing or empty values. As illustrated in the sequential pipeline in \textbf{Figure \ref{fig:SequentialPreprocessingPipeline}}, these gaps are handled based on the statistical variation of the feature. Depending on the feature's distribution, these gaps are addressed through mathematical substitution, such as applying the feature mean or using linear interpolation, or through the systematic removal of records showing excessive missing data to maintain dataset integrity.

Following the initial cleaning, noise and outlier handling mechanisms are applied to filter unusual data points generated by temporary sensor glitches or transmission errors. Algorithmic encoding is performed to translate categorical variables into numerical representations. The preprocessing pipeline concludes with comprehensive programmatic consistency checks to definitively verify the structural integrity and uniformity of the cleaned dataset. This final audit ensures that all processed records adhere to the predefined schema and that no data corruption occurred during the transformation stages. \subsection{Feature Engineering}
The primary objective of the feature engineering phase was to augment the predictive performance of the machine learning models by providing more nuanced and expressive data representations. Rather than relying exclusively on the raw soil properties captured through the sensor network and historical datasets, this phase involved mathematically deriving new variables that encode established agronomic relationships. By transforming raw measurements into high-order features, the system enables the algorithms to capture complex, non-linear dependencies between soil chemistry and plant health that are not explicitly present in the initial input vectors.

Two distinct categories of derived features were constructed for the engineered dataset. The first category comprised ratio-based features designed to quantify the balance between specific nutrients. The Phosphorus-to-Potassium ratio ($P/K$) was calculated to define the internal nutrient equilibrium between these two primary macronutrients, which serves as a critical fertility indicator in tropical soil profiles. Additionally, the Phosphorus-to-Organic Matter ratio ($P/OM$) was computed to express Phosphorus availability relative to the soil's organic composition, reflecting the established agronomic dependency of Phosphorus mobility on organic matter levels.

The second category consisted of interaction features, derived through multiplicative operations to encode conditional relationships. An Organic Matter $\times$ pH interaction term was generated to represent the principle that organic matter decomposition and subsequent nutrient release are strongly regulated by soil acidity. Similarly, a Potassium $\times$ pH interaction term was derived to capture the pH-dependent nature of Potassium uptake by root systems. These interaction terms allowed the model to learn complex interdependencies without requiring manual thresholding or excessively deep neural architectures.

The explicit encoding of these soil-crop relationships reduced the computational complexity required for the model to discover such patterns independently. By providing calculated nutrient ratios and pH-dependent interaction terms, the algorithm leveraged domain-specific knowledge directly, resulting in faster convergence during the training phase and improved generalization when processing unseen soil conditions. This engineering process successfully expanded the dataset from the four original raw variables to a comprehensive set of eight predictive features.

\subsection{Dataset Preparation for Machine Learning}
The primary objective of dataset preparation is to establish the final structural configuration of the preprocessed data, ensuring it is partitioned and formatted for the iterative training and evaluation cycles of the machine learning models. This phase represents the transition from static data storage to an active learning format, where information is structured to minimize bias and maximize the model's ability to generalize across diverse agricultural scenarios.

A fundamental component of this phase is dataset splitting, where the unified data is stratified to maintain representative class distributions across all subsets. As illustrated in \textbf{Figure \ref{fig:DataSplit}}, the dataset is divided into three definitive partitions: 70\% of the data is allocated for direct model training, 15\% is utilized for validation to systematically tune hyperparameters, and the remaining 15\% is strictly reserved for blind testing to evaluate final real-world performance.

\subsection{Data Storage and Management}
The architecture of the proposed system necessitates a centralized and reliable repository to ensure data consistency across administrative and end-user platforms. This repository facilitates the management of diverse entities, including farmer and administrator accounts, farm records encompassing geographic coordinates and land area, active farming sessions, and soil test readings. Furthermore, the database stores the outputs of the machine learning engine, specifically crop and fertilizer recommendations, alongside system telemetry and device status updates. While machine learning models are maintained as distinct training artifacts, all operational and longitudinal data are housed within the database to ensure that the web dashboard, mobile application, and backend services utilize a unified source of truth.

The system utilizes a standard relational database management system, specifically PostgreSQL, which is deployed via a managed cloud service to ensure high availability and automated maintenance. The backend Application Programming Interface (API) establishes a secure connection to the database, organizing data into structured tables for users, farms, sessions, soil tests, and recommendations. To optimize schema complexity, richer nested data structures are stored using structured fields within the relational environment, reducing the need for an excessive number of auxiliary tables while maintaining query efficiency. The mobile application interacts with this layer through standard HTTP requests, while the web application leverages a Representational State Transfer (REST) layer provided by the hosting service to streamline Create, Read, Update, and Delete (CRUD) operations without schema duplication.

Security and scalability are integral to the data management strategy of the system. User authentication is secured through strong cryptographic hashing for passwords, while session management is handled via a dual-token system consisting of short-lived access tokens and longer-lived refresh tokens. These tokens are stored within secure on-device storage on the mobile platform and verified against secrets maintained in the server configuration. Furthermore, the hosting environment implements Row-Level Security (RLS) policies to ensure that users are restricted to accessing only their authorized data subsets. To accommodate increasing traffic and data volume, the system employs connection pooling, stateless API instances capable of horizontal scaling behind a load balancer, and paginated data retrieval to minimize latency and server overhead. Consistent data integrity is maintained through automated backup protocols managed by the database provider, ensuring recovery in the event of system failure.

	The TANIM system employs a multi-stage data pipeline designed to transform raw environmental signals into actionable agricultural intelligence. This workflow integrates physical sensing, cloud-based data management, and advanced machine learning analytics to provide decision-support tools for both farmers and regional agricultural authorities. As illustrated in \textbf{Figure \ref{fig:DataProcessingWorkflow}}, the comprehensive data flow, from initial acquisition to end-user dissemination, follows a logical progression through hardware interfacing, cloud integration, and predictive modeling.
	
	\begin{figure}[H]

		\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/DataProcessingWorkflow.png}

	The primary stage of the workflow involves the high-fidelity collection of soil parameters at the physical layer. The system utilizes a multi-parameter soil sensor to monitor Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K), pH, moisture, salinity, and temperature, all of which are interfaced with an ESP32 microcontroller. To ensure data integrity during local transmission, a MAX485 module is employed to convert RS-485 differential signals to TTL-compatible logic for the microcontroller. These data packets are subsequently encapsulated and transmitted via the LoRa (Long Range) protocol to a central gateway. This modulation technique is specifically selected to ensure reliable connectivity in topographically challenging or remote agricultural zones where traditional cellular infrastructure is often insufficient. 
	
	Upon receipt at the gateway, the data is forwarded to a Firebase cloud database for centralized management and refinement. During this phase, the system performs rigorous data cleaning by executing filtering algorithms to mitigate electrical noise and remove statistical outliers from the raw sensor readings. Furthermore, a data fusion process contextualizes the real-time sensor data by integrating it with secondary datasets. These include meteorological information, such as precipitation and humidity acquired via external APIs, and geospatial zoning maps provided by the Department of Agriculture (DA) Region 10.
	
	The analytical core of the system leverages a LightGBM algorithm, a gradient boosting framework utilized for its high efficiency and superior performance with tabular agricultural datasets. The process begins with metaheuristic-based feature selection to identify the most significant soil and climatic variables, thereby reducing dimensionality and improving model generalization. The predictive engine then performs dual-task analytics, which include classifying soil health status based on established agronomic thresholds and forecasting crop suitability to optimize regional yield performance.

The figure \ref{fig:SystemArchitecture} shows the updated system architecture for the Soil Health Monitoring, Fertilizer, and Crop Recommendation System (TANIM). The system goes from hardware in the field, through a LoRa link and a local gateway, into the cloud for storage and machine learning, and then out to a web app for agricultural authorities and a mobile app for farmers.
Field IoT device. The sensing unit is basically one IoT package in the field. A solar panel feeds a solar panel manager, which handles charging and power so the device can keep running in areas that are far from the grid. Inside the box, an ESP32 reads the sensors, does any simple processing on the readings, and builds the packets that will be sent out.

A soil sensor measures things like NPK, pH, and moisture. The sensor talks over an industrial serial link that behaves like RS-485, which is good when the cable is long and the environment is noisy. A TTL MAX485 module turns that into a regular UART signal the ESP32 can read, so you get reliable communication without making the firmware too complicated.
The device also has a GPS module. The ESP32 reads the GPS data and sends location together with the soil readings. That way the cloud and the TANIM Web App can show maps and trends by area, not just a list of numbers with no place attached.

The ESP32 sends data through a LoRa module. LoRa is meant for low power and longer range, which fits farms and rural sites.
Gateway and path into the cloud. A LoRa receiver listens for packets from one or many nodes in the field. It is connected to a second ESP32 that works as a gateway. That ESP32 receives the LoRa traffic, checks the data, can hold or reshape packets if needed, and then sends everything upstream. The forwarded data includes soil readings, and GPS location.

Cloud, machine learning, and apps. After the gateway, the system splits into two main parts in the cloud. One part is machine learning, which uses new data and past data to look for patterns in the soil, spot possible problems like low nutrients or moisture stress, and suggest fertilizers, crops, and simple soil health takeaways. The other part is Supabase, which is the main backend for the project. Supabase stores data in a database, handles sign in and users, and exposes APIs for things like sensor history, profiles, and who is allowed to see what, especially for authority users.

The TANIM Web App is built for agricultural authorities. It uses Supabase for the underlying data and adds views like dashboards and maps, plus results from the ML side where those are wired in. The TANIM Mobile App is built for farmers in plainer language: soil insights, recommendations, alert. Both apps use Supabase for secure access to stored data and connect to the machine learning side when the app needs model based recommendations.

The Implementation phase involves developing the software and hardware components. \subsection{Software Implementation}
The software implementation for TANIM is designed to provide an efficient, reliable, and user-friendly platform that caters to the needs of both the Farmer and the Department of Agriculture. To ensure optimal accessibility and performance, the frontend for farmers is developed using React Native, a framework well-suited for creating responsive and cross-platform mobile applications. The mobile application also features offline data handling, allowing farmers to access previously synced soil and crop data even without an internet connection. Any new inputs or updates made while offline are temporarily stored locally on the device and automatically synchronized with the cloud once connectivity is restored. This ensures uninterrupted functionality in rural areas with limited network coverage.
	
Meanwhile, the frontend for the Department of Agriculture is implemented using React.js, providing a seamless and interactive web application experience. The web application integrates advanced analytics and visualization tools, enabling agricultural authorities to interpret soil data, monitor farm conditions, and analyze regional agricultural trends through interactive charts, dashboards, and geospatial maps. These insights support data-driven planning, resource allocation, and policy formulation.
	
Both user interfaces interact with a shared backend developed in Flask, which centralizes data management and ensures consistent functionality across platforms. This architecture enables efficient communication between the mobile and web applications while supporting scalability, maintainability, and ease of integration with additional features or services in the future.
	
\subsection{Hardware Implementation}
The hardware implementation of the TANIM system is centered on an IoT-based device designed to collect soil data and send it to the cloud for processing and analysis. The core of the device is the ESP32 microcontroller, selected for its high processing capability, built-in Wi-Fi and Bluetooth connectivity, low power consumption, and compatibility with multiple sensor modules. These features make it ideal for continuous field monitoring and data transmission in remote agricultural areas.
	
A soil sensor is used to measure key soil parameters such as pH, NPK, moisture, temperature, and salinity. This sensor was chosen because it provides accurate and real-time soil data essential for evaluating soil health and guiding fertilizer and crop recommendations.
	
\subsection{Machine Learning Model}
The TANIM system will use a boosting algorithm as the core of its predictive model, chosen for its efficiency, scalability, and high accuracy in handling large and complex datasets. The TANIM system secures the exchange of information across multiple layers of the architecture. For the long-range link between the field sensors and the gateway, AES-128 encryption is a standard feature of LoRa technology that is utilized to ensure that soil parameters remain confidential during wireless transmission. Furthermore, communication between the Flask backend and the user applications is conducted over HTTPS (TLS/SSL) to prevent data interception or man-in-the-middle attacks. This ensures that real-time insights, such as fertilizer recommendations and crop suitability scores, are delivered securely to the end-user.
	
Data integrity begins at the collection point, where the ESP32 microcontroller handles localized processing and filtering to prevent signal spikes from being recorded as valid data. The hardware is protected by a durable 3D-printed container, designed to secure the PCB and sensors from physical tampering in remote agricultural settings. On the cloud side, Firebase Security Rules are configured to ensure data isolation; Farmers are only permitted to write data associated with their specific accounts, while the Department of Agriculture is granted read-only access to regional soil health trends for monitoring purposes. Regular database backups and encryption-at-rest further safeguard the repository of environmental and farmer-defined parameters.
	
For wireless communication, the system uses a LoRa (Long Range) module, selected for its ability to transmit data over several kilometers with very low power consumption. This makes it suitable for rural agricultural zones with limited internet access or unstable connectivity.
	
The entire system is powered by a solar panel managed through a solar panel controller, ensuring a sustainable and uninterrupted power source even in off-grid farming locations. The modular design of the hardware also makes the system scalable and adaptable, allowing additional sensors or communication modules to be integrated easily as agricultural requirements evolve.
	
\section{Test and Evaluation}
Testing and evaluation will be conducted to verify the system’s effectiveness, accuracy, and usability for crop recommendation and soil health monitoring. This process will be designed to ensure that the hardware components, software components, and machine learning models will function according to what we aim for this study, which involves hardware testing and calibration, functional testing, system integration testing, performance testing, and usability testing. Hardware testing and calibration will focus on verifying the accuracy of the sensors to ensure reliable soil data. Functional testing will examine whether the mobile and web applications operate correctly and deliver the expected outputs of each feature. To ensure that the hardware, cloud database, machine learning, and applications works together with proper data flow and error handling, system integration will be implemented. For measuring the responsiveness of the system and to evaluate the system’s predictive accuracy of the machine learning models using various performance metrics we will be conducting performance testing. Moreover, usability testing will assess the practicality and user-friendliness of the system by involving farmers and agricultural experts in field trial and feedback collection.
	
\subsection{Hardware Testing and Calibration}
Hardware testing and calibration will ensure that the IoT sensors will provide accurate measurements of soil health parameters such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, pH, moisture, salinity, and temperature. To conduct this testing, the sensors will be deployed in field testing on the actual farms in Region 10, also to ensure consistency under varying field conditions the readings will be recorded at different time and day. Cross checking will be applied by using the Department of Agriculture’s soil testing laboratory and soil test kits of the same soil samples from the same farms to ensure reliability and accuracy.
	
Through statistical measures such as Mean Absolute Error (MAE) the sensor readings will be compared against laboratory results. a maximum allowable error threshold of ±5 will be set, if the difference between the sensor and laboratory values exceeds this threshold error, calibration adjustments will be applied to the sensors until acceptable accuracy will be achieved. It will be calculated by:
	\begin{equation}
		\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|
		\label{eq:mae}
	\end{equation}
	\myequation{Mean Absolute Error (MAE)}
	
This process ensures that the data generated by the hardware is reliable enough to be used in machine learning models and the recommendation system.
	
\subsection{Functional Testing}
Functional testing involves verifying whether each feature of the mobile and web applications operates correctly according to the system design. For the mobile application, the following features will be tested: secure login and authentication, retrieval of soil health readings, display of crop recommendations for multi-cropping, fertilizer optimization, and offline data synchronization. For the web application, features such as farm visualization, farmer profile management, and crop rule configuration will be tested.
	
In addition, synchronization between the database, mobile, and web applications will be tested to ensure that all data are accurately updated and consistent across platforms. This includes verifying that soil readings, user information, and crop recommendations are properly synchronized when data are sent or retrieved from the cloud.
	
Both black-box testing and white-box testing approaches will be applied to evaluate the system’s functionality. Black-box testing will focus on verifying the system’s external behavior without examining its internal code. Testers will input various data to check if the outputs match the expected results, ensuring that all functions operate as intended from a user’s perspective. Meanwhile, white-box testing will be conducted by examining the internal logic, data flow, and code structure of the applications. This will help identify hidden logical errors, broken functions, or inefficient code implementations.
	
Stress testing will also be performed by simulating multiple user logins and concurrent farm entries to evaluate system performance under heavy load. Through these testing activities, the TANIM system aims to ensure accurate functionality, reliable synchronization between components, and stable performance in real-world agricultural applications.
	
\subsection{System Integration Testing}
System integration testing will evaluate whether the hardware, software, cloud database, and machine learning model communicates seamlessly with each other as a single system. The system integration testing will be tested by following the full flow of the system: soil health readings captured by the sensor and processed by the ESP32 microcontroller, then transmitted by via the LoRa module to the gateway, forwarded to the cloud database, then processed by the machine learning models, and finally displayed on the mobile and web applications. 
	
This process confirms that the system works together as one reliable system. Furthermore, tests will be conducted to observe how the system handles incomplete or corrupted data to ensure that it can manage error to prevent misleading recommendations to farmers.
	
\subsection{Performance Testing}
For the system level performance, it is to determine if the system could handle multiple data acquisition and provide timely feedback to the users. The data transmission success rate will be monitored by comparing the successful sensor to cloud uploads against the attempted uploads over multiple trials. Latency tests measure the time from the sensor data capture to its display in the mobile and web application, with an acceptable range set to 30 seconds. Scalability will be evaluated by simulating multiple farms connected simultaneously to ensure that the cloud database and applications could handle increased workloads without delays and crashes. These will ensure that the system is reliable and efficient under real-world agricultural conditions where multiple users operate.
	
For the machine learning evaluation, it verifies the predictive capability of the machine learning models that will be used on crop recommendation for multi-cropping and fertilizer optimization. The dataset will be split into 70-15-15 for training, evaluation, and testing for maximizing the predictive accuracy and avoiding overfitting.

\subsection{Usability Testing}
	The usability testing will assess the practicality, accessibility, and user satisfaction of the system. For this, farmers will be asked to log into the mobile application, review soil health data, view crop and fertilizer recommendations, and simulate updating their farm records. Meanwhile, DA officials will use the web dashboard to access farmer profiles, view GIS-based maps, and manage crop rules.
	
	A total of 10 participants will be involved in the usability testing, consisting of 7 farmers from Region 10 and 3 agricultural technicians or DA employees. Farmers were selected based on their familiarity with basic smartphone usage and their active involvement in small- to medium-scale farming. The DA personnel were chosen because they represent the system’s administrative users responsible for monitoring and validating agricultural data. This mix of participants ensures that both end-user and administrative perspectives are considered during evaluation.
	
	After completing the navigation of the system, the System Usability Scale (SUS) developed by Brooke (1995) will be employed to quantitatively measure user satisfaction. The interactions of the users will be observed, and any difficulties they encounter will be recorded. In addition to the SUS scores, qualitative feedback will be collected through short interviews and observation notes to gain insights into which features are most useful and which aspects require improvement. The evaluation criteria will determine the user satisfaction based on the SUS scores and user feedback. The result of this testing will determine the system's usability rating and this will be a guide for further refinement to ensure that the TANIM system is accessible, user-friendly, and beneficial to the users.
